# Euclidean Algorithm Correctness and Termination

Let $D(a, b)$ denote the set of common divisors of integers $a$ and $b$.

## 1. Fundamental Invariant: Set of Common Divisors

Suppose $c \in D(a, b)$. By definition, $c \mid a$ and $c \mid b$. By the linear combination property of divisibility, $c \mid (a - b)$. Since $c \mid (a - b)$ and $c \mid b$, it follows that $c \in D(a - b, b)$. Therefore:

$$
D(a, b) \subseteq D(a - b, b)
$$

Conversely, suppose $c \in D(a - b, b)$. Then $c \mid (a - b)$ and $c \mid b$. Summing these terms yields:

$$
c \mid ((a - b) + b) \implies c \mid a
$$

Since $c \mid a$ and $c \mid b$, it follows that $c \in D(a, b)$. Therefore:

$$
D(a - b, b) \subseteq D(a, b)
$$

Since each set is a subset of the other, the two sets of common divisors are identical:

$$
D(a, b) = D(a - b, b)
$$

Because the set of common divisors is invariant under this reduction, their greatest elements must also be equal:

$$
\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(a - b, b)
$$

---

## 2. Generalization to Division with Remainder

By the Division Algorithm, for any integers $a$ and $b > 0$, there exist unique integers $q$ (quotient) and $r$ (remainder) such that:

$$
a = q b + r \quad \text{where } 0 \le r < b
$$

Rearranging this equality gives $r = a - q b$.

If $c \in D(a, b)$, then $c \mid a$ and $c \mid b$, which implies $c \mid (a - q b)$, so $c \mid r$. Thus $c \in D(b, r)$.

Conversely, if $c \in D(b, r)$, then $c \mid b$ and $c \mid r$, which implies $c \mid (q b + r)$, so $c \mid a$. Thus $c \in D(a, b)$.

This establishes the set equality $D(a, b) = D(b, r)$, which implies:

$$
\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, r) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)
$$

---

## 3. Proof of Termination

Consider the sequence of remainders $r_0, r_1, r_2, \dots$ generated by the division form of the Euclidean algorithm:

$$
\begin{aligned} r_0 &= a \\ r_1 &= b \\ r_0 &= q_1 r_1 + r_2 \quad &(0 \le r_2 < r_1) \\ r_1 &= q_2 r_2 + r_3 \quad &(0 \le r_3 < r_2) \\ &\;\;\vdots \\ r_{k-2} &= q_k r_{k-1} + r_k \quad &(0 \le r_k < r_{k-1}) \\ r_{k-1} &= q_{k+1} r_k + 0 \end{aligned}
$$

### Well-Ordering Principle

The non-negative remainders form a strictly decreasing sequence of non-negative integers:

$$
b = r_1 > r_2 > r_3 > \dots > r_k \ge 0
$$

By the **Well-Ordering Principle of Natural Numbers**, every non-empty set of non-negative integers contains a least element. A strictly decreasing sequence of non-negative integers cannot be infinite; it can contain at most $b + 1$ terms.

Therefore, there must exist some finite index $k+1$ where the remainder becomes zero ($r_{k+1} = 0$).

---

## 4. Proof of Correctness at Termination

When the algorithm terminates at $r_{k+1} = 0$, applying the invariant iteratively across all $k$ steps yields:

$$
\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(r_0, r_1) = \gcd(r_1, r_2) = \dots = \gcd(r_{k-1}, r_k) = \gcd(r_k, 0)
$$

By definition of the greatest common divisor, for any non-zero integer $d$:

$$
\gcd(d, 0) = d
$$

Because every integer divides 0 and the largest positive divisor of positive $d$ is $d$, we have $\gcd(r_k,0)=r_k$.

Thus, the last non-zero remainder $r_k$ is precisely $\gcd(a, b)$. This proves both the termination and mathematical correctness of the algorithm.
